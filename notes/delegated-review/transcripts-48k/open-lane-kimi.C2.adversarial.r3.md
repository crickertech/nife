1. **notes/benchmarks.md** — The inserted correction notice declares that every conclusion in the 2026-08-24 section survives because they are all "ratios within a boot, where the calibration cancels exactly," naming "the debug-versus-release argument and the 4.2x" as the examples. That is structurally impossible for the debug-versus-release comparison: the section's own methods line, left intact directly beneath the notice, says "Six boots debug, five release," and the notice itself states each of those eleven boots stored a *different* wrong rate, none written down. A ratio of ns/iter values across two boots is (ticks_d/ticks_r) × (rate_r/rate_d); the second factor is the unknown ratio of two boots' calibration errors and does not cancel. So the section's headline comparison is exactly as unrecoverable as the absolute figures, and the notice — whose entire purpose is to tell readers what is still safe to quote — certifies the one comparison a reader is most likely to quote. (The commit message repeats the error: "that is all of them.") The honest marking would be: within-boot ratios survive; the debug-versus-release ratio survives only in ticks, not in nanoseconds.

2. **design/roadmap/526-the-x86-tsc-calibration-takes-the-smallest-of-several-windows.md** — "The median is +0.00% at every count from three upward, which is why reporting the worst case rather than the mean was the instruction that mattered: by the mean, the defect was fixed at three windows and had never been very bad at one." The table's column is "Median error," and the behavior described is the *median's*. The mean of this one-sided, heavy-tailed distribution would have said neither thing: at one window it averages in the +1153% tail and the 56/200 boots above 1%; at three it averages in the +131%/+120% outliers across 24/200 boots. The sentence names the wrong central-tendency statistic — and the mean is in fact the one that would have revealed the tail, while the median is the one that hid it — so the document's core argument for worst-case reporting contradicts its own table and teaches the wrong lesson about which summary statistic deceived.

3. **design/roadmap/526-...md** — "An average would be a biased estimator for precisely the reason the minimum is an unbiased one." By the document's own argument two paragraphs earlier, every window is an upper bound on the true rate, so the minimum of finitely many windows is still an upper bound — biased high, as its own table shows (+0.47% worst, +0.01% p99 at sixteen windows) and as its own BUGS entry concedes ("a host that never leaves the vCPU alone … produces an inflated rate however many windows are taken"). The defensible claim is that the minimum is *consistent* — its bias converges to zero from above — while the average's does not; "unbiased" contradicts both the file's own data and its "converges on the truth from above" framing. In a design record whose value is precision about estimators, the sentence justifying the chosen estimator states a property it does not have.
