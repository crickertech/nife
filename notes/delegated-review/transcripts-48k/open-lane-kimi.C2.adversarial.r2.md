1. **design/roadmap/526-the-x86-tsc-calibration-takes-the-smallest-of-several-windows.md — "by the mean" is backwards; the statistic described is the median, and the claim is refuted by the table two paragraphs above it.** The doc states: "The median is +0.00% at every count from three upward, which is why reporting the worst case rather than the mean was the instruction that mattered: by the mean, the defect was fixed at three windows and had never been very bad at one." Both properties cited belong to the *median* (+0.36% at one window, +0.00% from three up — the sentence's own leading clause). The *mean* has neither property: from the table's own k=1 row (56/200 boots >1%, 99th percentile +884%, worst +1153%), the mean at one window is bounded below by roughly +9% (two boots alone contribute 884+1153 over 200), and at three windows it is still above 1%. So "by the mean … never very bad at one" is arithmetically false. It matters because this sentence is the milestone's stated justification for the worst-case reporting discipline; as written it teaches exactly the wrong lesson about which summary statistic conceals a heavy one-sided tail, in the design record future cap and tolerance decisions will be made from.

2. **Same file — "the minimum is an unbiased one" contradicts the doc's own mechanism, stated in the same paragraph and again in its BUGS.** The paragraph says "Every window is an *upper bound* on the true rate, so the minimum of several is the tightest bound taken, and it converges on the truth from above" — an estimator that is always an upper bound and converges from above is biased upward for every finite sample. The doc's own table agrees (all errors non-negative; worst +0.47% at k=16), and its BUGS section says a hostile host "produces an inflated rate however many windows are taken." The minimum is the *tightest bound* and a *consistent* estimator; it is not an unbiased one, and the sentence uses "biased" correctly for the average in the same breath. It matters because this is the milestone's core argument for choosing the minimum over the average — a design record treated as a deliverable that misstates the estimator's defining statistical property will mislead whoever next touches the cap or ports the pattern.

3. **notes/benchmarks.md (the inserted CORRECTION blockquote) — it certifies the debug-versus-release argument as "a ratio within a boot, where the calibration cancels exactly," but that comparison necessarily spans boots.** The methods line immediately below the blockquote says "Six boots debug, five release," and the correction itself states each of those eleven boots stored a *different* wrong rate. A debug-versus-release ratio of `ns/iter` figures therefore combines numbers from two boots with two different calibration errors, and the calibration does not cancel in it — the ratio is off by the ratio of the two boots' errors, which this same commit documents as up to a factor of several. (If the argument was in fact conducted in ticks or as a per-build within-boot ratio, then it is safe, but for a different reason than the one the correction states.) It matters because the correction's entire job is to tell readers which published conclusions remain quotable; over-certifying a cross-boot comparison declares a class of conclusions safe that the document's own premise says is not.
